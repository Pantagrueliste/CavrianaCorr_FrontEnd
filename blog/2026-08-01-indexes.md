**TL;DR** The edition now has an index of people and an index of places. Neither was compiled by hand: both are generated from the encoding, so neither can drift away from it.

<!-- truncate -->

Every person and place named in these letters already carried a pointer into an authority file — 1,129 references across the published transcriptions, resolving to 212 records, 62 of them with VIAF identifiers and 84 with Getty coordinates. The site printed all of it as plain text. The scholarship was in the files and nowhere on the page.

Two pages now expose it. [People](/people) and [Places](/places) list every entity the letters name, in index form, each entry gathering every letter that mentions it. Names in the transcription link to their entry, and the entry links back to the letters. The place index opens with a map of the Europe of 1570 rather than of today: Franche-Comté is Habsburg, Ferrara still belongs to the Este, Corsica to Genoa, and the Empire is divided into its ten Imperial Circles. Sixty-seven located places are plotted, sized by how often the letters name them, and clicking one goes to its entry. The calendar heatmap on the front page works the same way: click a day and you reach the letter written on it.

None of this is curated. A script reads the two authority files and every letter, and writes a single JSON file at build time. There is no database and no server — the indexes are static files, rebuilt whenever a letter is encoded. Minimal computing applies to indexes as much as to anything else.

The useful consequence is that an index generated from the encoding reports its own gaps. Mine currently shows twelve authority records that no published letter cites, and thirty-three references I have not yet identified. A hand-made index quietly conceals that sort of thing. This one publishes it, which is the more honest arrangement and, incidentally, my working list.
