<div class="letter-summary">

Cavriana writes to Bartolomeo Concino, first secretary of Cosimo I de' Medici, to unblock his admission to the Order of Santo Stefano and, in exchange, to offer himself formally as the Grand Duke's intelligence agent in France. He recalls two earlier letters, one unsigned because of the delicacy of the business and one signed, and explains that the ambassador Petrucci and the secretary Alberto have now asked him, on Concino's behalf, what exactly he wants. His proofs of nobility, sent to Pisa according to the rules supplied by Jacopo Dani, have been debated by the deputies for four months with no decision, while two of his countrymen have already received the habit without completing theirs; Alessandro Striggio reports that Enea Vaini found the proofs sound but that the Grand Master's own instructions are awaited. Cavriana insists that he wants to enter "by the door and not by the window": although he could produce letters from princes the Grand Duke would not wish to refuse, he asks the favour from Concino alone. He then states his real claim. He already serves without payment, keeping a paid servant in Germany and paid contacts among secretaries at the French court, at a cost that neither his salary nor his medical practice can meet, and he could go on serving without ever showing himself, to the benefit of newly arrived Tuscan ambassadors and secretaries. He is a physician "by force"; his true bent is for letters, affairs of state and religion. In two words: he wishes to live and die a servant of the Grand Duke, and asks the Grand Duke's favour as his recompense. If he is refused he will "set sail elsewhere", having already been pressed to serve others. He mentions the Latin history of the recent French war that he has written, with the designs of the princes and the manoeuvres of the captains, which Vincenzo Alamanni has partly seen but which he dares not circulate while those it describes are still alive. The letter is dated from Saint-Germain, 15 July 1570. The postscript on fol. 221r turns to news: nothing new from Tuscany, and the friends he wrote of are not stirring; the coming peace will expose designs that were quietly being put in place; the Corsicans are being solicited by "the common enemy" to let its agents in, but are holding firm — whence his joke, on Paul's authority, that all islanders are bad. In Piedmont the Pinerolo negotiation is under way, and given the state of pay, supplies and treachery there is reason to fear a surprise against those who idly enjoy the peace. The peace deputies sent by Admiral Coligny have reached Saint-Germain and a settlement cannot be long delayed. The German diet will probably not be held, since the princes summoned have refused to come and have settled matters among themselves. Once peace is made here, the troops are expected to be sent abroad, to purge France of its flood of brigands and idle men and keep it clean. He has had no word whether the earlier news pleased the Grand Duke, and asks Concino to tell him — and, above all, to burn his letters, since the world is full of malicious people and a man cannot guard against every accident.
